# Numeric scrutiny

Numbers carry unearned authority. A figure that is wrong, or right about
something other than what it is being used for, is harder to spot than a wrong
sentence — and more damaging, because it gets quoted. Run these checks on any
quantitative claim before recording it.

## Denominator and base rate

- **What is the denominator?** "Fraud rose 300%" is meaningless without the
  base. From 1 case to 4 is a 300% rise. Always find the absolute numbers
  behind a percentage, and the percentage behind absolute numbers.
- **What is the base rate?** A test that is "95% accurate" for a condition
  affecting 1 in 10,000 produces far more false positives than true ones. Any
  claim about detection, screening, or prediction needs the prevalence to be
  interpretable.
- **Per what?** Totals across populations of different sizes are not
  comparable. Check whether a per-capita, per-dollar, or per-unit-time
  normalization changes the ranking — it very often reverses it.

## Definitional swap

The most common quiet error in research synthesis. Two sources use the same
word for different quantities, and the numbers get compared as if they were
commensurable. Before treating two figures as conflicting *or* agreeing, check
that they measure the same thing:

- Same population, or a subset?
- Same time window — calendar year, fiscal year, trailing twelve months?
- Same geography and currency; nominal or inflation-adjusted?
- Same inclusion rule — "users" meaning registered, monthly active, or paying?
- Gross or net; before or after the adjustment the source applies?

If the definitions differ, the claims do not conflict — they are about
different quantities, and reporting them as a conflict is itself an error.
Record it as a definitional difference.

## Selection and survivorship

Ask what was filtered out before anyone measured:

- Survey respondents self-selected; who did not answer, and would they differ?
- Companies, funds, or products that failed and left the dataset
- Studies that were run but not published (publication bias) — if a finding
  rests on one positive result, look for a registry or a null-result search
- Benchmarks reported by the party that optimized for them

## Forecasts and growth

- A projection is a model output, not a measurement. Record the assumption it
  rests on, or the claim is unfalsifiable.
- Compound growth extrapolated more than a few years is a statement about the
  model, not the world.
- Check whether a "market size" figure is revenue, shipments, installed base,
  or addressable opportunity. These differ by orders of magnitude and are
  routinely conflated.
- Who produced the forecast, and do they sell into the market it describes?

## Units, scale, and arithmetic

Cheap checks that catch real errors: billion vs million; percent vs percentage
point (a rise from 4% to 6% is 2 percentage points, or 50%); currency and
year; absolute vs indexed values. If a claim includes components and a total,
add them. If it includes a rate and a base, multiply. Stated arithmetic fails
more often than you would expect.

## Uncertainty

A point estimate without an interval is a weaker claim than it appears. Look
for the confidence interval, margin of error, or range, and carry it into the
claim text. "Between 2% and 19%" should never be recorded as "about 10%".

When two sources give different numbers, check whether their intervals
overlap before calling it a contradiction. Overlapping intervals are
agreement, not conflict.

## What to record

Record the figure **with** its denominator, population, time window, units,
and uncertainty. A number stripped of its qualifiers is not a finding; it is a
future error. If the source does not state the qualifiers, that absence is
what to record.
